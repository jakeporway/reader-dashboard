I — The Fauvists

"Les Fauves" — The Wild Beasts

In 1905, a group of painters exhibited canvases so violently colorful that a critic looked at a Renaissance-style sculpture in the middle of the room and said: "Donatello parmi les fauves!" — Donatello among the wild beasts. The name stuck. The artists loved it.

The Fauves lasted only three years (1905-1908) as a movement — making them one of the shortest and most explosive revolutions in art history. Their radical idea? Color doesn't have to describe reality. It can describe feeling.

A face could be green. A tree could be orange. The sky could be whatever the painter felt it should be. This sounds obvious now. In 1905, it was like someone detonating a bomb in a cathedral.

"How do you see these trees? They are yellow. So, put in yellow. This shadow, rather blue? Paint it with pure ultramarine. These red leaves? Put in vermilion."
— Paul Gauguin to Paul Sérusier, 1888 (the seed of Fauvism)
Henri Matisse
The Open Window, Collioure
1905 · National Gallery of Art, DC
This is where it all started. Matisse spent a summer in the fishing village of Collioure with André Derain, and they dared each other to push color further and further. Look at the walls — they're pink, green, violet simultaneously. The boats are dashes of pure pigment. He painted what he felt standing at that window, not what a camera would record. Matisse later said this painting was "the first to express clearly, with pure colors, my emotions."
Henri Matisse
The Dance (I)
1909 · MoMA, New York
This is at MoMA — go find it. Five figures dancing in a circle on a green hill against a blue sky. Three colors, zero detail, infinite energy. Matisse stripped away everything except pure movement and joy. Notice how the two figures on the left have broken hands — the circle is almost complete but not quite. Art historians argue about whether this represents the ecstasy of connection or its impossibility. Matisse never explained.
André Derain
Charing Cross Bridge, London
1906 · National Gallery of Art, DC
Derain painted London the way nobody had seen it: the Thames is bright blue and yellow, Big Ben is violet, the bridge is green. He was directly competing with Monet, who had painted the same bridge in soft, misty impressionist light. Derain's version screams: "Your London is polite. Mine is alive." Monet reportedly saw Derain's London series and was troubled by it.
🧠 Why Fauvism matters for how we see everything today
Fauvism broke the rule that color must serve description. Once that rule was broken, everything followed: Cubism (break form), Abstract Expressionism (break representation), Pop Art (break seriousness). Every Instagram filter, every movie color grade, every brand color palette exists in a world the Fauves made possible. They didn't just change painting — they changed the idea that perception is fixed. You don't see the world as it is. You see it as you are.
Henri Manguin
Le Golfe
c.1905 · Bemberg Foundation, Toulouse
Manguin was called "the sensual Fauvist" — his colors were bold but warm. Vlaminck was the wildest of the wild beasts, but Manguin was the most seductive. He squeezed paint directly from the tube onto the canvas — no palette, no mixing. He once said: "I want to burn down the École des Beaux-Arts with my cobalts and vermilions." He'd been a bicycle racer and a violinist before becoming a painter. He painted like he was still racing.

II — The Impressionists

The Original Rebels (Who Look Respectable Now)

Here's the thing nobody tells you: the Impressionists were the punk rockers of their era. They were rejected, mocked, and literally named as an insult. Today their paintings sell for $100 million and hang in corporate lobbies. They'd be horrified.

Before the Impressionists, "serious" painting meant historical scenes, myths, and portraits — done indoors, in studios, with precise lines. The Impressionists said: what if we just went outside and painted what light actually looks like?

This was revolutionary. Not because nobody had painted outdoors before, but because they insisted that a fleeting moment — sunlight on water at 4pm on a Tuesday — was as worthy of art as the Battle of Waterloo.

Claude Monet
Water Lilies
1906 · Art Institute of Chicago (series at MoMA)
MoMA has several Water Lilies — find them. Monet painted roughly 250 water lily paintings over the last 30 years of his life. He was going blind. As his vision deteriorated, the paintings became larger, more abstract, more radical. The late ones — which MoMA has — are essentially abstract expressionism 50 years early. He couldn't see details anymore, so he painted pure sensation. His disability became his innovation.
Claude Monet
The Japanese Footbridge
1899 · National Gallery of Art, DC (variant at MoMA)
Monet didn't just paint his garden — he designed it first, then painted it. He hired six full-time gardeners. He diverted a river to create the lily pond. He ordered specific water lilies from Japan. The paintings are not observations of nature — they're observations of a designed experience. He was a UX designer who worked in chlorophyll.
"I perhaps owe having become a painter to flowers."
— Claude Monet
Vincent van Gogh
The Starry Night
1889 · MoMA, New York
This is THE painting. Go stand in front of it. Van Gogh painted this from the window of his room at the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in Saint-Rémy, France. He was there voluntarily after cutting off part of his ear. Here's what's wild: the swirling sky isn't hallucination. In 2004, astronomers confirmed that the turbulence patterns in Starry Night mathematically match real fluid dynamics turbulence — something scientists didn't formalize until the 1940s. A man in a mental asylum, painting at night, accidentally captured the mathematical structure of the cosmos.
💰 Van Gogh sold ONE painting in his lifetime
In his entire life, Van Gogh sold exactly one painting: The Red Vineyard, for 400 francs (about $2,000 today). He made roughly 2,100 artworks. He was supported entirely by his brother Theo, who believed in him completely despite zero market validation. Today, his paintings sell for over $100 million each. The market was wrong for 100 years. Theo was right from day one.
Paul Cézanne
The Large Bathers
1906 · Philadelphia Museum of Art (Cézanne works at MoMA)
Cézanne is the bridge. He took Impressionism's interest in perception and pushed it toward geometry — "treat nature by the cylinder, the sphere, the cone." Without Cézanne, there is no Cubism. Without Cubism, there is no modern art. Picasso called him "the father of us all." Matisse called him "a sort of god of painting." Both meant it literally.

III — The Connection

How the Impressionists Became the Fauvists

The timeline matters. The Impressionists broke subject matter — paint what you see, not what you're told to see. Then the Post-Impressionists (Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin) broke form — paint what you feel, not just what you see. Then the Fauves broke color — let it scream, let it clash, let it be wrong.

Each generation took the previous one's revolution and said: "You didn't go far enough."

🔗 The specific moment it happened
In 1901, a young Henri Matisse saw a Van Gogh retrospective in Paris. He was electrified. Van Gogh's thick, emotional brushwork and non-naturalistic color showed Matisse that color could carry pure emotion without depicting anything "correctly." Four years later, Matisse painted Woman with a Hat using green and purple for her face — and Fauvism was born. The straight line runs: Monet → Van Gogh → Matisse. Impressionism → Post-Impressionism → Fauvism. Each one a dare to go further.

IV — While You're Here

Don't Leave MoMA Without Seeing These

⭐ Cannot Miss
The Starry Night — Vincent van Gogh
Floor 5 · Galleries 1-2
You already know. But stand close and look at the thickness of the paint. Some strokes are a quarter-inch high. He wasn't painting on the canvas — he was sculpting with it.
⭐ Cannot Miss
Les Demoiselles d'Avignon — Pablo Picasso
Floor 5 · Gallery 2
Five women. Angular faces. Two with African mask features. Painted in 1907, this is the moment art broke. Picasso's friends thought he'd gone insane. Matisse thought it was a hoax. It was the birth of Cubism, and nothing was ever the same. The two figures on the right were painted AFTER Picasso visited an ethnographic museum — you can see exactly where his mind changed mid-painting.
⭐ Cannot Miss
The Persistence of Memory — Salvador Dalí
Floor 5
The melting clocks! It's TINY — only 9.5 by 13 inches. Everyone expects it to be huge. Dalí said the melting watches were inspired by Camembert cheese melting in the sun. That's either genius or a lie. Probably both.
🎨 For the Fauvist Fan
The Dance (I) — Henri Matisse
Floor 5
Five dancing figures, three colors, pure ecstasy. Already described above — but see it in person. It's almost 9 feet wide. The energy is physical.
🎨 For the Fauvist Fan
The Red Studio — Henri Matisse
Floor 5 · Gallery 2
Matisse painted his entire studio in a single flat red. Furniture, walls, floor — all the same Venetian red. Only the artworks within the studio have other colors. It's a painting about painting. The room dissolves; the art remains. MoMA bought it in 1949 and it changed how an entire generation understood color.
💫 Surprise Yourself
Campbell's Soup Cans — Andy Warhol
Floor 4
32 canvases, 32 flavors. Warhol's question: if Monet can paint 250 water lilies and it's genius, why can't I paint 32 soup cans? He's not mocking art. He's asking: who decides what deserves attention? Same question the Impressionists asked 100 years earlier.
💫 Surprise Yourself
Christina's World — Andrew Wyeth
Floor 3
A woman crawling through a field toward a farmhouse. Most people assume she fell. She didn't — Christina Olson had a degenerative muscle condition and this was how she moved. Wyeth painted not a tragedy but her determination. She refused a wheelchair. The distance between her and the house is the whole painting.
💫 If You Have Time
MoMA Sculpture Garden
Ground Floor · free to enter from the lobby
Even if you're art-fatigued, step outside into the garden. Picasso, Rodin, and others in open air. Sit on a bench. Breathe. Museums are marathons disguised as strolls.

The Wild Beasts Were Right

Color is emotion. Perception is personal. The "wrong" way to see something is sometimes the most honest way. The Impressionists gave us permission to look. The Fauvists gave us permission to feel.

Enjoy the rest of MoMA. Take your time. And if something stops you in your tracks — something you didn't expect — that's the painting that was meant for you today. 💜
